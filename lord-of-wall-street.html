Lord of Wall Street

Brooklyn, 1985. A garage, a desk, a phone, and a mentor who thinks you might be worth her time. Build the firm. Keep it alive until 2100.

What you do

You are Lou Street. Every week the office hums along: your people work, the phone rings, and the Gazette lands on the desk. Every so often the clock stops and a window asks you something — a hire, a client, an inspector at the door, a tip that is probably too good.

Your products are funds. For each one you pick an asset class, a strategy and a vehicle, then let your team build it. Any combination is allowed; a bad one is simply rated badly by the judges. High yield is a strategy, not a vehicle, and the game will tell you so once, in one sentence, when it matters.

Mackenzie

“Three rules, Lou. Never run out of cash. Never bore your clients. And never, ever, trust a tip from a man in a limousine.”

Mackenzie, your mentor, who has seen two crashes and expects a third.

1985 to 2100

Five eras, eight offices, from the garage to the top floor. The early years follow real history; the later ones belong to a future we wrote for the game. The free part of the game runs from January 1985 to Black Monday, October 1987 — more than an hour of play, and a proper ending.

1985

A garage in Brooklyn. Pizza nights, a second-hand terminal, your first fund.

1986

Clients arrive, and so do the inspectors. Risk control stops being optional.

1987

Black Monday. The free part of the career ends here, one way or another.

2000s onward

Bigger offices, bigger funds, bigger falls. New industries nobody knows how to price.

Losing, and coming back

The Wall Street Gazette

Street Capital closes its doors after a disastrous quarter. Sources say the founder was last seen reloading an earlier save.

Firms go under. When yours does, the game offers you the last yearly save, so a catastrophe costs a year rather than the career. The rare trophies are not handed out for showing up.
